What do these terms mean?
Sunlight
Time the Sun is above the local terrain and there is no total eclipse. A low Sun can still give a flat solar panel very little light.
Earth in view
Time the center of Earth is above the terrain. An actual radio link also needs antennas and a receiving station. A satellite can relay messages from the far side.
Both together
Time when the Sun and Earth are in view at the same moment. This can help a mission schedule solar-powered work that needs direct contact.
Local skyline
The outline of hills and crater rims from the landing site. A nearby ridge can hide the Sun even when it is above the Moon’s flat reference horizon.

            Data & methods

            What the ranking means

            Astralis filters out sites that miss your requirements. It scores qualifying sites using the weighted share of mission time with Sun, Earth, and both visible. Inspect the longest gaps before choosing a site.

            How visibility is estimated

            JPL’s DE440 and lunar orientation kernels drive both views. The 3D scene uses hourly geometric positions. Surface operations use 15-minute apparent positions. The model checks each object's center against the skyline from 2 m above the surface and excludes periods of total solar eclipse. January 2024 through December 2030 is supported.

            Global LOLA terrain is sampled at 16 pixels per degree, about 1.9 km north to south. Eight default polar sites use the finer 80 and 240 m LOLA grids.

            How to interpret the result

            Sunlight is a power opportunity, and Earth visibility is a communication opportunity. Power also depends on the solar array. Actual communications depend on the antenna, Earth station, and link budget. Study points illustrate different terrain. Landing hazards require a separate assessment.
